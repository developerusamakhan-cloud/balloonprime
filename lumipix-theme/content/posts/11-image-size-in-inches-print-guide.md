Screens think in pixels. Printers think in inches and centimetres. When a photo prints blurry, or comes out the wrong size, it's nearly always because the two weren't matched up. This guide turns image size in inches and centimetres into pixels, and gives you a simple way to get it right every time.

## The one formula you need

**Pixels = inches x DPI**

DPI (dots per inch) is how many pixels go into each inch of paper. For sharp photo prints, use **300 DPI**. For posters you look at from across the room, 150 DPI is usually plenty.

For centimetres, divide by 2.54 first: **pixels = (cm divided by 2.54) x DPI**.

## Print sizes at 300 DPI

| Print size | Inches | Pixels needed |
|---|---|---|
| Wallet | 2.5 x 3.5 in | 750 x 1050 |
| Standard photo | 4 x 6 in | 1200 x 1800 |
| 10 x 15 cm | 3.94 x 5.91 in | 1181 x 1772 |
| 5 x 7 | 5 x 7 in | 1500 x 2100 |
| 8 x 10 | 8 x 10 in | 2400 x 3000 |
| A4 | 8.27 x 11.69 in | 2480 x 3508 |
| A3 | 11.69 x 16.54 in | 3508 x 4961 |

Most phone cameras take 12 megapixels or more (around 4000 x 3000 pixels), which is enough for a sharp 8 x 10 print at 300 DPI.

## How to resize an image in inches or centimetres

1. Open the page to [resize image into inches](tool:resize-image-in-cm) and centimetres, and drop your photo in.
2. Set the **Unit** to inch, cm or mm.
3. Set **DPI** to 300.
4. Type the print size, say 6 x 4 inches. The line underneath shows the pixels you'll get.
5. If the photo's shape doesn't match the paper, choose **Fill** to cover the print, or **Fit** to keep the whole picture with a border.
6. Save as **JPG** and download. The DPI is saved in the JPG, so print software knows the size you meant.

## Why prints get cropped

A typical phone photo is 4:3. A 4 x 6 print is 3:2, a 5 x 7 is 5:7, and an 8 x 10 is 4:5. When the shapes don't match, the print service either crops your photo or adds white borders. Resizing it yourself with **Fill** or **Fit** means you decide what gets cut, not the print lab.

## Can a higher DPI make a print sharper?

Only partly. Changing the DPI number without adding pixels just changes the print size. The same 1200 x 1800 image is 4 x 6 inches at 300 DPI, or 8 x 12 inches at 150 DPI. Enlarging the pixels can smooth things a bit, but it can't add real detail. If you want a big print, start with the biggest original you have. Not sure what yours can handle? ID photos are the most common small print. A 35 x 45 mm passport photo needs 413 x 531 pixels at 300 DPI. ## Centimetre sizes at 300 DPI

Outside the US, prints are usually sold in centimetres. Here are the common ones:

| Print size | Pixels at 300 DPI | Pixels at 150 DPI |
|---|---|---|
| 9 x 13 cm | 1063 x 1535 | 531 x 768 |
| 10 x 15 cm | 1181 x 1772 | 591 x 886 |
| 13 x 18 cm | 1535 x 2126 | 768 x 1063 |
| 15 x 21 cm | 1772 x 2480 | 886 x 1240 |
| 20 x 30 cm | 2362 x 3543 | 1181 x 1772 |
| 30 x 40 cm | 3543 x 4724 | 1772 x 2362 |

The 150 DPI column is handy for posters and canvases people look at from a distance.

## How far away people stand changes everything

The further away someone is, the fewer pixels per inch they can actually see. That's why billboards look fine from the road even though they're very low resolution up close.

| Print | Typical viewing distance | Enough DPI |
|---|---|---|
| Photo album, passport photo | In your hand | 300 |
| A framed print on a desk | About 50 cm | 200 to 300 |
| A poster on a wall | 1 to 2 metres | 150 |
| A large banner | Several metres | 50 to 100 |

So a 12 megapixel phone photo that only gives you 13 x 10 inches at 300 DPI can still make a good A2 poster at 150 DPI.

## Getting a photo ready for a print shop

1. **Ask the shop for the exact size and bleed.** Some prints need a few millimetres of extra image around the edges, which gets trimmed off.
2. **Resize to the size plus bleed** in mm or inches at 300 DPI.
3. **Choose Fill** so the photo covers the whole area, and check the crop keeps heads and important details away from the edges.
4. **Save as JPG** at high quality, unless the shop asks for something else.
5. **Put the size in the file name**, like `holiday-10x15cm.jpg`, so the shop knows what you ordered.

If the shop has an upload limit, you can [compress image to 2MB](tool:compress-image-to-2mb) and still keep nearly all the detail.

## Documents follow the same maths

An A4 page scanned at 300 DPI is 2480 x 3508 pixels. If a document prints too small or too big, check its DPI. A scan saved at 72 DPI will print huge unless you set the size again. Our guide on [how to check if a photo is 300 DPI](post:how-to-check-if-photo-is-300-dpi) shows where to find the number, and you can [change image DPI](tool:change-image-dpi) without touching the pixels.

## Mixing photos of different shapes

When you print a set of photos at the same size, some will be landscape, some portrait, and the shapes may not match the paper. Decide for each one:

- **Fill** for most photos, accepting a small crop.
- **Fit with a white border** for photos where every edge matters, like group shots.

Doing it before you order saves you surprises when the prints arrive. Sharing the same photos online too? The [Instagram image sizes guide](post:instagram-image-sizes) lists the screen sizes to use.

## The short version

- **Pixels = inches x DPI.** For centimetres, divide by 2.54 first.
- **300 DPI** for photos held in the hand. 150 DPI is fine for posters.
- **Match the print's shape** with Fill or Fit to avoid surprise crops.
- **Save the DPI in the file** by exporting a JPG from a resizer that supports print units.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many pixels is a 4x6 photo?

At 300 DPI, a 4 x 6 inch print needs 1200 x 1800 pixels.

### What DPI should I use for printing?

300 DPI for photos you look at up close. 150 to 200 DPI is fine for big posters seen from a distance.

### How do I resize an image to A4?

Set the unit to mm, the DPI to 300 and the size to 210 x 297 mm, or use 2480 x 3508 pixels.

### How many pixels do I need for an A4 print?

At 300 DPI, A4 (210 x 297 mm) needs 2480 x 3508 pixels. For a poster seen from a distance, about half that is usually fine.

### What is bleed in printing?

Bleed is a few millimetres of extra image around the edges that the printer trims off, so you don't get thin white lines at the edge.
