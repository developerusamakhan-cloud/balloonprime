Screens think in pixels. Printers think in inches and centimetres. When a photo prints blurry, or comes out the wrong size, it is almost always because the two were not matched. This guide gives you the numbers and a simple method.

## The one formula you need

**Pixels = inches × DPI**

DPI (dots per inch) is how many pixels are squeezed into each inch of paper. For sharp photo prints, use **300 DPI**. For posters viewed from a distance, 150 DPI is usually enough.

For centimetres, divide by 2.54 first: **pixels = (cm ÷ 2.54) × DPI**.

## Print size chart at 300 DPI

| Print size | Inches | Pixels needed |
|---|---|---|
| Wallet | 2.5 × 3.5 in | 750 × 1050 |
| Standard photo | 4 × 6 in | 1200 × 1800 |
| 10 × 15 cm | 3.94 × 5.91 in | 1181 × 1772 |
| 5 × 7 | 5 × 7 in | 1500 × 2100 |
| 8 × 10 | 8 × 10 in | 2400 × 3000 |
| A4 | 8.27 × 11.69 in | 2480 × 3508 |
| A3 | 11.69 × 16.54 in | 3508 × 4961 |

Most phone cameras capture 12 megapixels or more (around 4000 × 3000 pixels), which is enough for a sharp 8×10 print at 300 DPI.

## How to resize an image in inches or centimetres

1. Open the [Image Resizer](tool:image-resizer) and drop your photo.
2. Change the **Unit** to *inch*, *cm* or *mm*.
3. Set **DPI** to 300.
4. Type the print size, for example 6 × 4 inches. The hint below shows the resulting pixels.
5. If the photo's shape is different from the paper, choose **Fill – crop edges** to fill the print, or **Fit – add background** to keep the whole picture with a border.
6. Save as **JPG** and download. The DPI is written into the JPG so print software knows the intended size.

## Aspect ratio: why prints get cropped

A typical phone photo is 4:3. A 4×6 print is 3:2. A 5×7 is 5:7, and 8×10 is 4:5. When the shapes differ, the printing service either crops your photo or adds white borders. Resizing yourself with **Fill** or **Fit** lets you decide what gets cut, instead of leaving it to the print lab.

## Can you increase the DPI to make a print sharper?

Only partly. Changing the DPI number without adding pixels just changes the print size: the same 1200 × 1800 image is 4×6 inches at 300 DPI or 8×12 inches at 150 DPI. Enlarging the pixels (upscaling) can smooth the result a little but cannot add real detail. If you need a large print, start with the largest original you have.

## Passport and ID photos

ID photos are the most common small print. A 35×45 mm passport photo needs 413 × 531 pixels at 300 DPI. Our [passport photo size guide](post:passport-photo-size-guide) covers the rules, and the [passport photo maker](tool:passport-size-photo) sets everything up for you.

## Centimetre print sizes at 300 DPI

Outside the United States, prints are often sold in centimetres. Here are the common ones:

| Print size | Pixels at 300 DPI | Pixels at 150 DPI |
|---|---|---|
| 9 × 13 cm | 1063 × 1535 | 531 × 768 |
| 10 × 15 cm | 1181 × 1772 | 591 × 886 |
| 13 × 18 cm | 1535 × 2126 | 768 × 1063 |
| 15 × 21 cm | 1772 × 2480 | 886 × 1240 |
| 20 × 30 cm | 2362 × 3543 | 1181 × 1772 |
| 30 × 40 cm | 3543 × 4724 | 1772 × 2362 |

The 150 DPI column is useful for posters and canvases that people look at from a distance.

## How viewing distance changes what you need

The further away people stand, the fewer pixels per inch they can see. That is why billboards look fine up close at very low resolution.

| Print | Typical viewing distance | Enough DPI |
|---|---|---|
| Photo album, passport photo | Hand-held | 300 |
| Framed print on a desk | Around 50 cm | 200–300 |
| Poster on a wall | 1–2 metres | 150 |
| Large banner | Several metres | 50–100 |

So a 12-megapixel phone photo that only gives 13×10 inches at 300 DPI can still make a good A2 poster at 150 DPI.

## Preparing a photo for a print shop

1. **Ask the shop for its exact size and bleed.** Some prints need a few millimetres of extra image around the edges that get trimmed.
2. **Resize to the size plus bleed** with the [Image Resizer](tool:image-resizer) in mm or inches at 300 DPI.
3. **Choose Fill** so the photo covers the whole area, and check the crop keeps heads and important details away from the edges.
4. **Save as JPG** at high quality, unless the shop asks for another format.
5. **Name the file with the size**, for example `holiday-10x15cm.jpg`, so the shop knows what you ordered.

## Printing documents at the right size

Scanned documents follow the same maths. An A4 page scanned at 300 DPI is 2480×3508 pixels. If a document looks too small when printed, check its DPI: a scan saved at 72 DPI will be printed huge unless you set the size again. Our guide on [how to check if a photo is 300 DPI](post:how-to-check-if-photo-is-300-dpi) shows where to find the value.

## Mixing photos of different shapes

When you print a set of photos at the same size, some will be landscape and some portrait, and their shapes may not match the paper. Decide for each photo:

- **Fill** for most photos, accepting a small crop.
- **Fit with a white border** for photos where every edge matters, such as group shots.

Doing this yourself before ordering avoids surprises when the prints arrive. If you are also sharing the same photos online, the [Instagram image sizes guide](post:instagram-image-sizes) lists the screen sizes to use.

## Key takeaways

- **Pixels = inches × DPI.** For centimetres, divide by 2.54 first.
- **300 DPI** for photos held in the hand; 150 DPI is fine for posters.
- **Match the print's shape** with Fill or Fit to avoid surprise crops.
- **Write the DPI into the file** by saving as JPG from a resizer that supports print units.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many pixels is a 4×6 photo?

At 300 DPI, a 4×6 inch print needs 1200 × 1800 pixels.

### What DPI should I use for printing?

300 DPI for photos viewed up close. 150–200 DPI is fine for large posters viewed from a distance.

### How do I resize an image to A4?

Set the unit to mm, the DPI to 300 and the size to 210 × 297 mm, or use 2480 × 3508 pixels.

### How many pixels do I need for an A4 print?

At 300 DPI, A4 (210×297 mm) needs 2480×3508 pixels. For a poster viewed from a distance, half that is usually fine.

### What is bleed in printing?

Bleed is a few millimetres of extra image around the edges that the printer trims off, so there are no white slivers at the edge.
