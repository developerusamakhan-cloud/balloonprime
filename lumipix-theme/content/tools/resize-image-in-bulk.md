## Batch resize without installing software

Resizing photos one at a time is slow. Product shots for an online shop, holiday photos for a gallery, screenshots for a report: they usually all need the same width or the same frame. This tool applies one set of options to a whole batch, so 20 images take about as long as one.

Everything runs in your browser. Your photos are processed on your own computer or phone and are never uploaded, which keeps the tool fast on slow connections and safe for private pictures.

## How to resize images in bulk

1. **Drop your photos** onto the box, tap **Choose files**, or paste with Ctrl+V. You can add up to 20 images at once.
2. **Set the size.** Type a width in pixels, tap a preset such as Full HD or Instagram portrait, or switch the unit to percent, cm, mm or inches.
3. **Choose how shapes are handled.** Keep the aspect lock on to give every photo the same width while each one keeps its own proportions. Turn it off to give every photo the exact same frame.
4. **Pick a format** if you need one, such as JPG for smaller files or PNG to keep transparency.
5. **Download** each image, or use **Download all (ZIP)** to save the whole batch in one file.

## Same width or same frame?

Bulk resizing works in two ways, and choosing the right one avoids stretched or cropped surprises.

- **Same width, own height** (aspect lock on). Every image becomes, for example, 1200 pixels wide. A landscape photo ends up shorter than a portrait one, and nothing is cropped. This is the right choice for blog posts, galleries and documents.
- **Same frame** (aspect lock off). Every image becomes exactly, for example, 1080 × 1080 pixels. Choose **Fill** to crop the edges so each photo fills the frame, or **Fit** to keep the whole photo and add a background colour around it. This is the right choice for product grids and social media.

The stretch option also exists, but it distorts faces and objects, so it is best avoided for photos.

## Good sizes for common jobs

| Job | Suggested size |
|---|---|
| Blog and website images | 1200 to 1600 px wide |
| Full HD slides and screens | 1920 × 1080 px |
| Online shop product photos | 1000 × 1000 to 2000 × 2000 px |
| Instagram portrait posts | 1080 × 1350 px |
| Email attachments | 1600 px on the long side |
| 4 × 6 in prints | 1800 × 1200 px at 300 DPI |

Resizing to a sensible width is also the easiest way to make files smaller. A 4000-pixel phone photo resized to 1600 pixels usually drops to a fraction of its original size.

## Resize by percent

Switch the unit to **%** to scale every image by the same amount, for example 50% to halve each side. This keeps the relative sizes of your photos, which is handy when a batch mixes large camera pictures and smaller screenshots.

## Resize in cm or inches for printing

For a batch of prints, switch the unit to cm or inches and set the DPI, usually 300. Each JPG gets the right number of pixels and the DPI is written into the file, so print software uses the size you chose. The [resize in cm and inches](tool:resize-image-in-cm) page explains print sizes in more detail.

## Tips for large batches

- Start from the original files. Images that were already shrunk by a messaging app have lost detail that resizing cannot bring back.
- Keep batches to 20 images. For more, clear the list and run the next batch with the same settings.
- Check one result before downloading the ZIP. The size under each file shows the final pixels and file size.
