## Is my photo 300 DPI?

That question usually comes up right before a print order or an upload to a form that says "minimum 300 DPI". The answer has two parts, and most people only get one of them. The first is the DPI value written inside the file. The second, which matters more, is whether the image has enough pixels for the size you want to print. This checker shows both, for up to 20 images at once, without changing or uploading anything.

## Where DPI is stored

A JPG can carry its DPI in two places: the JFIF header and the EXIF data. Different programs read different places, which is why Windows Properties and an online form can sometimes disagree about the same photo. The checker tells you which source it found. PNG files store the value in a block called pHYs. Many files, including most screenshots and images saved from websites or messaging apps, have no DPI at all. In that case the result says **No DPI stored**, and apps fall back to their own default, usually 72 or 96.

## Why pixels matter more than the DPI number

DPI is only a label that sets the print size. It does not add or remove detail. A 600 × 400 pixel image tagged 300 DPI prints sharply at just 2 × 1.33 inches; a 3000 × 2000 pixel image tagged 72 DPI can print sharply at 10 × 6.67 inches once its DPI is set to 300. That is why the most useful line in the results is the largest size the image prints sharply at 300 DPI. Our article on [what DPI means](post:what-is-dpi) covers the idea in more depth.

## What to do with the result

- **Enough pixels, wrong DPI value.** The image is fine; only the label is off. Set it to 300 with [Change image DPI](tool:change-image-dpi). For JPG and PNG this rewrites the value without re-compressing the photo.
- **Not enough pixels for your print.** Choose a smaller print size, or find a larger original. To prepare a file for an exact size such as 10 × 15 cm, use [Resize image in cm](tool:resize-image-in-cm), but remember that enlarging adds pixels, not real detail.
- **No DPI stored.** Common and usually harmless. If a print shop or form insists on a value, set one with the Change DPI tool.
- **The image is only for screens.** Ignore DPI completely. Websites, phones and social apps display pixels, not inches.

## Common problems and fixes

- **Another app shows a different DPI.** Programs read different headers, and some show their own default when nothing is stored. The checker names the exact source it found, so you know which value the file really carries.
- **A photo from my phone shows 72 DPI or none.** That says nothing about its quality; phone photos usually have plenty of pixels. Check the largest sharp print line instead of the DPI value.
- **A downloaded image says No DPI stored.** Websites and apps can drop this information when they save or compress images. It does not mean the image is low quality.
- **The verdict says the image is too small.** DPI cannot fix that. Use a bigger original or print smaller.
