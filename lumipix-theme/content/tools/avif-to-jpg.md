## Why turn AVIF into JPG?

AVIF is a modern image format that many websites use because it keeps files small. The problem appears when you save one of those images: older photo viewers, some editing programs, online forms and print services may not accept a .avif file. JPG is the opposite. It is old, but almost everything opens it. Converting gives you a copy that works wherever you need to send it, and the whole job happens on your own device.

## How to convert AVIF to JPG

1. **Drop your AVIF files** onto the page, click to choose them, or paste a copied image with Ctrl+V. You can add up to 20 at once.
2. Check that **Convert to** is set to **JPG**. It is preselected on this page.
3. Leave **Quality** at the default of 92, or move the slider (1–100) to balance file size against detail.
4. If any image has transparent areas, pick a **Background** colour. White is the default.

Every result shows the old size, the new size and the pixel dimensions, so you can see what changed before you save anything.

## Browser support for AVIF

Because the conversion runs in your browser, the browser itself must be able to decode AVIF. Current versions of Chrome, Edge and Firefox can, and so can Safari 16.4 or newer. If an AVIF file refuses to open, update your browser or try another one on the same device. There is nothing extra to install.

## What changes and what stays the same

| Property | After converting to JPG |
|---|---|
| Pixel dimensions | Unchanged: same width and height |
| Transparency | Filled with the **Background** colour you choose |
| Colours | Converted to sRGB |
| EXIF metadata (camera model, GPS) | Removed |
| Animation | Only the first frame is kept |
| File size | Depends on the **Quality** setting |

JPG has no transparency, so any see-through areas in the AVIF need a solid colour. White suits documents and most uploads. If the image will sit on a coloured web page or slide, choose a colour that matches it so the edges blend in.

## Choosing a quality setting

The **Quality** slider controls how strongly the JPG is compressed. The default of 92 keeps photos looking clean at a sensible file size. Higher values keep more fine detail and produce bigger files. Lower values shrink the file, and at very low settings you may start to see blocky areas or smeared edges, especially around text and sharp lines.

| Quality | Good for |
|---|---|
| 90–100 | Photos you plan to edit, print or archive |
| 80–90 | Sharing, email and most websites |
| 60–80 | Quick previews and messaging |
| Below 60 | Only when a strict size limit forces it |

Do not be surprised if the JPG is larger than the AVIF you started with. AVIF usually compresses more efficiently than JPG, so a JPG of similar visual quality often needs more bytes. If you need the result under a specific size, convert first, then use a target such as [compress to 100KB](tool:compress-image-to-100kb) or the general [Image Compressor](tool:compress-image).

## Common problems and fixes

**The file will not load.** Your browser may be too old to decode AVIF. Update it, or open this page in a current version of Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari 16.4+.

**Transparent parts turned white.** That is expected, because JPG cannot store transparency. Pick a different **Background** colour, or switch to PNG or WebP to keep the transparent areas.

**An animated image became a still.** Only the first frame of an animated file is converted, so the result is a single still JPG.

**The JPG looks soft or blocky.** Raise **Quality** and convert again from the original AVIF.

**The file is bigger than expected.** Lower **Quality** a little, or compress the result afterwards. If the image also has far more pixels than you need, make it smaller with the [Image Resizer](tool:image-resizer).

**The photo lost its location data.** Re-encoding removes EXIF metadata such as the camera model and GPS location. Many people see this as a bonus before sharing images online.

## AVIF, JPG and the other choices

JPG is the safe choice for compatibility: photos, email attachments, forms and printing. PNG is better for screenshots, graphics and anything that needs transparency. WebP sits in between and works well on websites. For a closer look at the trade-offs, read [WebP vs JPG](post:webp-vs-jpg) and [PNG vs JPG](post:png-vs-jpg). If your files are WebP rather than AVIF, the [WebP to JPG converter](tool:webp-to-jpg) works the same way.

## Where AVIF files come from

You will mostly meet AVIF when saving pictures from websites, because many sites serve it to keep pages light. Some cameras, phones and editing apps can also export it. In each case the image looks perfectly normal in the browser, but the saved file may be rejected by an app or upload form that only expects JPG or PNG. Converting is the quickest way around that.
