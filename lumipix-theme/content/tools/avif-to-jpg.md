## Why turn AVIF into JPG?

AVIF is a newer image format, and lots of websites use it because the files are tiny. The trouble comes when you save one. Older photo viewers, some editing programs, online forms and print services may refuse a .avif file. JPG is the opposite: it's old, but almost everything opens it. Converting gives you a copy that works wherever you need to send it, and the whole thing happens on your own device.

## How to convert AVIF to JPG

1. **Drop your AVIF files** onto the page, click to pick them, or paste a copied image with Ctrl+V. Up to 20 at once.
2. **Check Convert to** says **JPG**. It's already selected on this page.
3. **Leave Quality at 92**, or move the slider (1 to 100) to trade file size against detail.

## Does my browser support AVIF?

Because the conversion runs in your browser, the browser has to be able to read AVIF. Current Chrome, Edge and Firefox can, and so can Safari 16.4 or newer. If a file won't open, update your browser or try another one on the same device. There's nothing to install.

## What changes and what stays the same

| | After converting to JPG |
|---|---|
| Pixel dimensions | The same width and height |
| Transparency | Filled with the **Background** colour you choose |
| Colours | Converted to sRGB |
| EXIF metadata (camera model, GPS) | Removed |
| Animation | Only the first frame is kept |
| File size | Depends on the **Quality** setting |

JPG has no transparency, so any see-through areas need a solid colour. White works for documents and most uploads. If the image will sit on a coloured page or slide, pick that colour so the edges blend in.

## Which quality?

The slider controls how hard the JPG is compressed. The default of 92 keeps photos looking clean at a sensible size. Higher keeps more fine detail and makes bigger files. Lower shrinks the file, and at very low settings you'll start to see blocky patches or smeared edges, especially around text.

| Quality | Good for |
|---|---|
| 90 to 100 | Photos you'll edit, print or keep |
| 80 to 90 | Sharing, email and most websites |
| 60 to 80 | Quick previews and messaging |
| Under 60 | Only when a strict size limit forces it |

Don't be surprised if the JPG is bigger than the AVIF. AVIF packs images more tightly than JPG, so a JPG that looks the same often needs more bytes. If you need it under a set size, convert first, then [compress image to 100KB](tool:compress-image-to-100kb) or use the general [image compressor](tool:compress-image).

## If something goes wrong

**The file won't load.** Your browser may be too old to read AVIF. Update it, or open this page in current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari 16.4 or newer.

**Transparent parts turned white.** That's expected, because JPG can't store transparency. Pick a different background colour, or switch to PNG or WebP.

**An animation became a still.** Only the first frame is converted, so you get one still JPG.

**The JPG looks soft or blocky.** Raise the quality and convert again from the original AVIF.

**The file is bigger than you wanted.** Lower the quality a little, or compress the result. If it has far more pixels than you need, shrink it with the [image resizer](tool:image-resizer).

## AVIF, JPG and the rest

JPG is the safe bet for photos, email, forms and printing. PNG is better for screenshots, graphics and anything with transparency. WebP sits in between and works well on websites. For more on the trade-offs, read [WebP vs JPG](post:webp-vs-jpg) and [PNG vs JPG](post:png-vs-jpg). If your files are WebP rather than AVIF, you can [convert WebP to JPG](tool:webp-to-jpg) the same way, and for PNG screenshots there's a page to [turn PNG to JPG](tool:png-to-jpg).

You'll mostly run into AVIF when you save pictures from websites, because many sites use it to keep pages light. Some cameras, phones and editing apps can export it too. The image looks perfectly normal in the browser, but the saved file gets turned away by apps and forms that only expect JPG or PNG. Converting is the quickest way round that.
