## Passport and ID photos at the right size

Passport, visa and ID photos follow strict rules, and size comes first. This tool crops and resizes any photo to standard sizes such as 35×45 mm and 2×2 inches at 300 DPI, ready to print or upload. It runs in your browser, so your photo never leaves your device.

## How to make a passport photo

1. **Take a suitable photo** (see the tips below).
2. **Drop it here** or tap **Choose files**.
3. **Pick a preset.** The photo is cropped from the centre to the right proportions.
4. **Download** the JPG. The 300 DPI value is written into the file so it prints at the exact size.

If the crop cuts too close to your head, use a photo taken from slightly further away, so there is more space around you.

## Taking a good photo at home

- **Plain, light background**, usually white or off-white; check your authority's rules.
- **Soft, even light** from a window in front of you, with no shadows on the face or wall.
- **Camera at eye level**, about 1–1.5 metres away, using the rear camera.
- **Neutral expression**, mouth closed, eyes open, looking straight at the camera.
- **No filters or retouching**, and glasses off if the rules require it.

## Printing your photo

Most photo shops print on 4×6 inch (10×15 cm) paper. You can place several passport photos on one sheet in a photo editor, or print a single photo and trim it. When printing at home, set the scale to **100%**, not "fit to page", so the photo comes out at exactly 35×45 mm or 2×2 inches.

## What this tool does and does not check

The tool handles **size, proportions and resolution**. It does not check biometric rules such as head size within the frame, expression, lighting or background colour. Those are your responsibility, and requirements differ between countries and offices. Always read the latest official guidance before submitting.

## Why 300 DPI?

Photo printing uses about 300 dots per inch for sharp results at hand-held distance. A 35×45 mm photo at 300 DPI needs 413×531 pixels; almost any modern phone photo has far more than that, so the result is crisp. The DPI value tells the printer how large to print the image. For more, read [how to check if a photo is 300 DPI](post:how-to-check-if-photo-is-300-dpi).

## Sizes in pixels for online applications

Some online applications specify the photo in pixels rather than millimetres. If you are given pixel dimensions, use the [Image Resizer](tool:image-resizer) with the unit set to **px**, turn the link off, type the numbers and choose **Fill**. ## Checklist before you print or upload

| Check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Correct size preset | The wrong proportions are rejected immediately |
| Face centred, looking at the camera | Most rules require a straight, front-facing pose |
| Plain, even background | Shadows and patterns are common reasons for rejection |
| No filters or retouching | Altered photos may be refused |
| Recent photo | Many authorities require a photo taken in the last six months |
| Sharp at 100% zoom | Blurry photos are rejected |

## Photos for children and babies

Rules for children are often slightly relaxed on expression and eye position, but background and lighting still matter. For babies, lay them on a plain white sheet and photograph from directly above. Then crop with the passport preset as usual.

## Saving money on prints

A standard 4×6 inch print costs much less than a passport photo service. At 300 DPI, a 4×6 inch sheet (1200×1800 pixels) fits up to six 35×45 mm photos. Arrange them on one sheet in a photo editor, leaving small gaps to cut along, and print at 100% scale.
