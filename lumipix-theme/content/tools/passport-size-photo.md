## Taking a good photo at home

- **A plain, light background**, usually white or off-white. Check what your authority asks for.
- **Soft, even light** from a window in front of you, with no shadows on your face or the wall.
- **Camera at eye level**, about 1 to 1.5 metres away. Use the back camera and ask someone to help.
- **A neutral face.** Mouth closed, eyes open, looking straight at the camera.
- **No filters or retouching**, and glasses off if the rules say so.

## Printing several copies on one sheet

Printing one tiny photo is a waste of paper and money. Under **Print sheet**, choose **10 x 15 cm** or **4 x 6 in photo paper** and the tool tiles as many copies as fit, with a small gap and a light cut line around each one. A 35 x 45 mm photo gives you six copies. A 2 x 2 inch photo gives two. Print it like a normal photo at a pharmacy, a photo shop or at home, then cut along the lines.

At home, set the printer scale to **100%** or "actual size", never "fit to page". Otherwise your photo won't come out at exactly 35 x 45 mm.

## What this tool checks, and what it doesn't

It handles **size, proportions and resolution**. It doesn't check the biometric rules, like how big your head is in the frame, your expression, the lighting or the exact background colour. Those are down to you, and they vary between countries and offices. Always read the latest official guidance before you submit.

## Why 300 DPI?

Photo printing uses about 300 dots per inch for prints that look sharp in your hand. A 35 x 45 mm photo at 300 DPI needs 413 x 531 pixels, and nearly any phone photo has far more than that, so the result is crisp. The DPI number tells the printer how big to print. For more, see [how to check if a photo is 300 DPI](post:how-to-check-if-photo-is-300-dpi).

Your photo is processed entirely on your device. Nothing is uploaded or stored, there's no account and no watermark, and you don't need to install anything.

## When the form gives pixels instead of millimetres

Some online applications give the photo size in pixels. In that case, open the [image resizer](tool:image-resizer), set the unit to **px**, turn the link off, type the numbers and choose **Fill**. Common examples are 600 x 600 for US applications, or 413 x 531, which is 35 x 45 mm at 300 DPI.

## Checklist before you print or upload

| Check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The right preset | Wrong proportions get rejected straight away |
| Face centred, looking at the camera | Most rules want a straight, front-facing pose |
| A plain, even background | Shadows and patterns are a common reason for rejection |
| No filters or retouching | Edited photos may be refused |
| A recent photo | Many authorities want one taken in the last six months |
| Sharp at 100% zoom | Blurry photos get rejected |

## Photos of children and babies

The rules for children are often a bit more relaxed on expression and where they're looking, but the background and light still matter. For a baby, lay them on a plain white sheet and take the photo from straight above. Then crop with the passport preset as usual.
