1. **Drop an image** onto the page or click to choose one. Up to 20 at a time.

## Getting a good result

Pixel art works best when the subject is simple and clear:

- **Crop close to the subject** first with the [crop image tools](tool:crop-image). A face or a pet that fills the frame turns out much better than a small subject in a busy scene.
- **Use a plain background,** or remove it first with [remove background for free online](tool:background-remover) so only the subject is turned into pixels.
- **Start with Retro colours** and only drop to Minimal if you want a very bold, poster style look.
- **Try a few pixel sizes.** The result updates as you move the slider.

## Full size or one pixel per block?

| Output | What you get | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Same as original | A big image where each block is a solid square | Posting online, prints, wallpapers |
| One pixel per block | A tiny image, one pixel for each block | Game sprites, editing in pixel art apps, icons |

For example, a 1600 pixel wide photo with 16 pixel blocks becomes a 100 pixel wide sprite. You can open it in any pixel art editor and touch it up by hand. If you need it bigger later without blur, scale it up by a whole number such as 4x or 8x in your editor with smoothing turned off.

## Why PNG is the default

Pixel art lives on sharp edges and exact colours. PNG keeps both. JPG softens the edges between blocks and slightly shifts the colours, which spoils the look. Choose JPG only if you need a small file and do not mind that. Transparent areas stay transparent in PNG, so a cut out subject keeps its clear background.

## How the colours are reduced

Each block's colour is the average of the pixels inside it. With a colour limit, every channel of that colour, red, green and blue, is rounded to a few fixed steps. Four steps per channel gives 64 possible colours, three steps gives 27 and two steps gives 8. That is why the Retro setting looks like early home computer graphics and Minimal looks like a bold poster.
